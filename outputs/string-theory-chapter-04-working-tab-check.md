Outside the music school, David's steps found Celeste's in the snow and kept to them across the quad. For a time the two sets of prints ran together beneath the trees, though she was already gone; then hers turned toward the bridge while his descended into the dark fold of Lower East Hill.

At the fork he watched her tracks continue three or four paces without him. Then he made himself look downhill.

The walk home took ten minutes. Eighteen months had worn it almost entirely from his awareness. There had once been conversation in those blocks, or silence that held meaning. Now he left rehearsal and found himself at the apartment door with no clear sense of what had occupied the interval. Tonight, after everything the day had already taken from sequence, even that familiar absence unsettled him.

The tall frosted-green door shut behind him with a hollow cough. Beyond the narrow entry hall, the two-story room waited as he had left it: brick rising the full height of the far wall, the fireplace dark beneath its mirror, the spiral staircase winding toward the loft and the grated bridge above. Across from the kitchen, three counterweighted chalkboard panels remained folded into the wall, presenting a blank white surface where an hour's damage could be concealed in seconds.

The apartment was not warm, but neither was it clinical. Its unfinished intentions—jagged brick, steel, glass, the chalk dust worked permanently into the baseboards—had always suited him. Other people remarked that the place detested company. With David it seemed merely to close around him, asking nothing.

Sheet music and loose staff paper colonized every available surface. A B-minor scale sprawled across the coffee table. David set the cello beside the staircase and stood in the kitchen, unable to decide whether the hollowness beneath his ribs was hunger or nausea.

The refrigerator offered a grease-glossed pizza box from two nights earlier. Three dried pepperoni slices remained inside. He gave them to the microwave, ate the first standing at the island and carried the other two to the couch.

When he sat, the notebook slipped from the arm beside him and fell open across the cushion. By the time the pizza had cooled again, its pages carried diagrams of harmonic-oscillator eigenstates and the jagged calculus of vibrato dynamics. His handwriting pitched forward, cutting through one line and then another until the paper tore beneath the pen. The small rupture brought the cello string back with the force of the original snap.

The day returned by sensation rather than order: the iron railing frozen into his palm; Arthur's watch bright against his wrist; the dry metallic trace of Elliot in the corridor; 68.48 Hz working through his jaw; Roger's hand receiving his elbow; Celeste, barely audible beneath the Adagio, asking him to stay.

He wrote 68.48 at the center of the page and circled it until the pen broke through.

To establish a baseline — to confirm that the day had occurred in the order he remembered it — he pulled the satchel onto the couch and drew out his lecture notes. He knew what they contained. The prepared spine of the morning: causality, the sum over histories, the pivot he had never reached. He had prepared them with the rest of the week's classes before leaving for the funeral, or he remembered preparing them, which until this moment had been the same thing.

Slowly, he reached his left hand toward the side of the leather binder, still mindful of the morning’s neurological glitches. His fingers gripped the corner and opened to the front page of…

Doodles. Spirals. Nothing. He turned the page. No outline. No number spine. Just spirals drawn over and over again, looping in and out, looking forward. Looking back.

Davids heart raced. He flipped the next page. Nothing. Again. Nothing. Again. Again. A–

The crude sketch o sitting on the  page before him stabbed off the page. David flung it violently to the ground, his body braced entirely in reaction and disbelief. There, staring at him from the floor, lay the page. On it, the sketch of a large square with another square perhaps half an inch larger surrounding. Inside its frame, a tuning fork, its prongs facing up out of the ground. 

David stood frozen in a swirling, seething heat of confusion, of pure entropy for all the day had piled over him. With a face as dead as he could hold, he walked away. Went straight into the kitchen, fetched a rocks glass from one cupboard and a bottle of scotch from another. Poured two fingers and drank it down one gulp. Then repeated the action. Then slammed the glass onto the counter with dangerous force. The noise of it screamed through the loft. As walked away, the sudden noise of shattering glass made him jump. He looked back to see the glass shattered all over the counter. Shattered, easily 15 seconds after the glass had been released.

His hand was shaking violently by the time he reached for his phone. Without granting himself enough time to choose otherwise, he pressed the impossible favorite.

Arthur's photograph filled the screen. The call routed once, twice, and then his father's voice entered the room with its old precise courtesy.

"Hello, this is Arthur Lang. I can't come to the phone at present. Please leave a message after the tone, and I'll return your call at the earliest interval."

The tone sounded.

"Dad," David said.

Nothing followed it. He ended the call.

The phone went dark in his hand.

David looked toward the mirror above the mantel and found his father's eyes set unmistakably within his own face. The brow, the narrowing at the corners, the lines beside the mouth that grief and age had lately begun to deepen: features he had spent years treating as incidental now clamored forward in Arthur's absence. The resemblance had not changed. Only David's ability to look away from it had failed.

He crossed to the mantel and placed two fingers against the glass. For one flickering moment the reflection divided—his father's face beneath, his own laid thinly over it—and the touch seemed to answer against his actual cheek.

Then the light shifted. There was only David.

He pulled down the first chalkboard panel. The counterweight moved somewhere overhead, followed by the familiar low complaint of cable and track.

At first he wrote only what he could defend: 68.48 Hz. 3:2. Fifteen minutes, thirteen seconds. Five minutes, maybe more. Three minutes, forty-seven seconds. He stopped before writing twenty years. The numbers refused to become an argument.

His best work had always lived in the narrow shimmer between tremolo and theorem. On the board, every attempt to hold that interval collapsed toward one side or the other: music stripped of the thing that made it human, mathematics contaminated by desire. He wrote until the chalk warmed between his fingers and the white dust entered the seams of his clothes.

By 2:47 a.m., the room had become a triangle of potentials. He stood equidistant from the cold stove, the chalkboard wall and the cello case. Each a vertex, none resolved.

The refrigerator compressor droned a perfect fourth above the bathroom faucet's drip. Chalk dust shimmered in the light from the vertical window, suspended like cosmic background radiation.

Outside the brick, beyond the city’s pall of light, earth sounded its eternal B-flat, waiting for a listener desperate enough to mistake melody for meaning.

The first page held no lecture outline.

A spiral occupied the center of the paper, drawn in black ink with sufficient pressure to emboss the sheet beneath it. The line wound inward, narrowed, and vanished into its own dark center.

David turned the page.

Another spiral.

This one opened outward.

On the next page, two had been drawn over one another, their curves intersecting so densely that the center had been rubbed nearly through.

He turned another page, then another.

No equations. No numbered sequence. No prepared transitions. Only spirals, repeated with small, deliberate variations: contracting, expanding, doubled, reversed. Some appeared to turn forward. Others seemed to look back.

His thumb stopped against the edge of the paper.

He listened to the apartment.

The refrigerator motor had fallen silent. Somewhere in the bathroom, the faucet released a single drop.

Blank.

The next was blank as well.

He moved more quickly now, searching for the point at which the lecture resumed, though he had begun to understand that it would not.

Blank.

Blank.

David knew it before he understood why he knew it.

As a child he had believed his father’s face belonged entirely to his father. He had not understood that the dead were patient. They waited in the blood. They entered by degrees: a gesture of the hand, the angle of the head when listening, the particular silence used in place of forgiveness.

The mirror was cold.

“You live inside a machine,” he had said on his first visit.

David had laughed. Arthur had looked pleased with himself for less than a second, then asked where he was supposed to hang his coat.
