## Scene-by-scene

**13.1 — The Monochord (Pythagoras).** David, twenty-eight hours into an unbroken working stretch in his wrecked study, strikes his tuning fork (A-432) after a Hamlet-adjacent phrase arrives unbidden ("If it be not now, then it may never come"). The room dissolves into a sunlit Mediterranean courtyard. Pythagoras demonstrates the monochord — plucked string, fundamental, then the perfect fifth overtone — and marks ratios with an ivory wedge on the string, gesturing toward a carved slab: *All things move in harmony.* David returns to his study to find the chalkboard equation advanced in a hand not his own — cleaner ratios bent toward string curvature. He strikes the fork again; the whole room resonates in sympathy and his own heartbeat settles into a perfect fifth against it.

**13.2 — the salon (Émilie du Châtelet).** A second strike of the fork (mid-chalk-stroke) drops David into a candlelit Enlightenment salon. Du Châtelet, mid-composition at a writing desk, works an abacus beside her foolscap, then plays a harpsichord figure built in doubling thirds. She states, half to the room: *"Le carré de la vitesse, c'est le doublement de l'octave"* — velocity squared is the doubling of the octave — and *"Comme si la nature préférait les puissances de deux à tout autre"* (as if nature preferred powers of two to all else). This is her real historical position: du Châtelet argued for *vis viva* (kinetic energy ∝ v²) against the Newtonian-Cartesian momentum-only camp, and translated Newton's *Principia* into French. The chapter renders her actual physics as a musical-harmonic revelation. David returns to find the equation's hardest transition solved in a lighter, unfamiliar hand.

**13.3 — The Purge.** No historical visitor — the human cost scene. David, in post-vision clarity, violently empties his office (rug, books, lamp, a "sea lion-shaped eraser," a fossilized sandwich under a chair cushion) into the hallway, then marches into a colleague's live lecture to reclaim a second chair to match the one he stole nine months earlier — mid-class, in front of thirty students and **Professor Garrin**, who is left stranded across the room from his own phone and has to sidestep the whole width of it before he can call the Dean. Rune Ishikawa visits after, needles David about the hallway wreckage, then genuinely praises the new equation transitions ("you might actually be cited by someone outside your usual fan club") — and drops a folded *Ithaca Times* on the desk with a circled headline: **"AS EXTREME SOLAR EVENT APPROACHES EARTH, NASA SCIENTISTS PINPOINT DATE: NOVEMBER 28, 2026."** Rune's parting small talk — "you know Carl was born in November... strange, the things we remember" — plants the Sagan/numerology beat without David (or the reader) yet registering its weight. David closes the scene finding what he's seen "beautifully terrifying."

**13.4 — Resonance (Helmholtz/Chladni).** That night, cello in hand, a newly restrung open A produces an overtone David has never heard. The room cuts — no dissolve this time — to a gaslit acoustics laboratory. A composite historical-scientist figure (dialogue and apparatus draw on both Hermann von Helmholtz's resonance theory and Ernst Chladni's sand-plate experiments — a deliberate blend, not a single attributed person) states in German: *"Das Ohr ist ein Frequenzanalysator. Aber das Universum ist eine Symphonie der Resonanzen"* (the ear is a frequency analyzer, but the universe is a symphony of resonances), then hands David a fork to strike against a Chladni plate. The sand resolves into a pattern David recognizes as the contour of a quantum state. David returns and composes through the night by retrieval rather than invention — "the music was not his... what appeared when he stopped trying to compose." He catalogs what he now carries: three figures, three demonstrations, three gifts — and one sensed absence in the sequence, a fourth shape not yet arrived.

**13.5 — minimums (Isaac Newton).** David strikes the fork harder, deliberately, and lands in a cold, plague-year London study — 1665/66, the Great Plague evacuation, Newton alone with a prism and foolscap. Newton demonstrates the visible spectrum, muted by the era's weak winter sun, and names the dark absorption lines in it (Fraunhofer lines, centuries before their formal 1814 discovery — the novel's anachronism is deliberate). He connects his own unbroken productivity directly to solar silence: the Maunder Minimum, seventy years without flares or aurora, "the sun quiet... in that silence, I heard the laws." He identifies David as the inverse case — born under a loud, magnetically turbulent sun — and has him strike the fork at a lower, stranger register that makes the spectrum's dark lines glow and the study wall crack into raw unobserved dark. David returns clutching the fork so hard the prongs draw blood, and finds on his desk a shard of "seventeenth-century silence" — a fragment that produces a small localized night wherever it's held to light. Three artifacts now: charcoal (13.1), foolscap (13.2), and this shard (13.5). The chapter closes on David hearing the city's ordinary hum acquire a new bass tone, an aurora pulsing 3:2 against his own heartbeat, and a laugh that is "layered... the sound of a man who had at last, in a life of ratios and regret, found an harmonic home."
