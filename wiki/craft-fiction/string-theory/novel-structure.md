# String Theory — Novel Structure

*String Theory* is a novel by Gabriel McPherson. At 107,550 words across an Overture and 20 chapters, it uses classical musical forms as structural scaffolding — chapters are subtitled as fugues, suites, passacaglias, and symphonies, mapping musical architecture onto narrative architecture. (source: string-theory-chapter-index.md)

## Structure at a Glance

The novel opens with an Overture framed as "a fragment recovered from sealed archive," establishing the text as recovered material. The 20 chapters vary widely in length, from the 293-word Overture to the 11,967-word Chapter 1.

## Musical Form as Structural Logic

The chapter subtitles are not decorative. Each named form carries compositional implications:

- **Fugue** (Ch. 2): a subject stated by one voice, taken up and developed by others. An "epistemic fugue in four voices" suggests four competing epistemological positions pursued in counterpoint.
- **Passion** (Ch. 3): the oratorio form — a narrative of suffering with formal devotional structure. "Secular passion" strips the theology but retains the form.
- **Suite** (Ch. 4): a sequence of contrasting movements with "walking interstitials," suggesting transitional passages between sections.
- **Fantasia quasi sonata** (Ch. 5): formally free but gesturing toward sonata structure; quasi signals approximation rather than strict adherence.
- **Symphony** (Ch. 6): the largest orchestral form, implying broad scope and multiple thematic groups.
- **Fantasia on Bach's Sarabande** (Ch. 11): variation or elaboration on a specific existing work — Bach's Sarabande from the Partita or French Suite tradition.
- **Passacaglia with chorale** (Ch. 16): ostinato bass with a repeating harmonic-melodic line above it; the chorale adds a hymn-like countermelody. The climactic formal choice.
