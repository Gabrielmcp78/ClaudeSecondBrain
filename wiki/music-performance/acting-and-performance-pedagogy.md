# Acting and Performance Pedagogy

*Gabriel McPherson's theatrical training and performance pedagogy is a structured methodology for actors. It integrates classical text analysis, active intention mechanics, and professional discipline to develop authentic, character-driven performances.* (source: Acting 3 Syllabus copy.pdf, Analyze This! U2.L1/L2.pdf, Monologue Rubric v1.pdf)

---

## 1. Classical Text Analysis & The First Folio

The pedagogy prioritizes text-first analysis, particularly when approaching Shakespearean and classical dramatic works:
- **Speech Truth**: Actors are trained to discover an integral sense of truth and believable human speech patterns by analyzing the punctuation, capitalization, and spelling in the First Folio of Shakespeare's works.
- **Avoidance of Artifice**: The goal is to allow the text to affect the actor's emotions naturally, avoiding high "classical" artifice or "singing" the verse. 
- **Script Analysis**: Utilizing detailed script analysis to generate authentic character choices that remain grounded in the text's given circumstances rather than external actor choices.

## 2. Intention Mechanics: Objectives and Tactics

The core acting technique maps a character's internal desires to physical onstage action through a two-part framework of Objectives and Tactics:

### Character Objectives
An objective is the primary driver of a character's actions onstage. It must be phrased using a specific interpersonal formula:
$$\text{Objective Formula} = \text{"I want [person] to do [thing I want them to do]."}$$

Key rules for objectives:
- **Interpersonal Direction**: An objective must always go *through* another person onstage. Vague desires (e.g., "I want food" or "I want an A") are incorrect because they lack relational friction. They must be rephrased relationally (e.g., "I want my boyfriend to make me dinner" or "I want my teacher to change my grade to an A").
- **Persistence**: An actor plays their objective until they achieve it or until the scene ends. If the objective is met, the character immediately pursues a new one.

### Character Tactics
Tactics are the methods a character employs onstage to get what they want from others.
- **Active Verbs**: Tactics must always be active verbs (action words describing behavior), such as *begging*, *coaxing*, *pleading*, *guilt-tripping*, *manipulating*, *threatening*, or *persuading*.
- **Tactical Dynamics**: Actors learn to recognize when a tactic fails and dynamically switch to a new verb to maintain pressure on their scene partner.

## 3. Monologue Performance Rubric

Evaluations are graded across seven distinct performance metrics to measure stage readiness:

1.  **Comprehension**: Demonstrating a thorough understanding of the text's subtext and bringing it to life.
2.  **Memorization**: Delivery of fully memorized text easily and without prompt assistance.
3.  **Focus**: Staying fully committed to the performance without breaking or being distracted by the audience or external elements.
4.  **Characterization**: Portraying a believable character with nuance, utilizing clear objectives and distinct tactical shifts.
5.  **Vocal Delivery**: Achieving clear projection, articulation, and vocal expression that matches the internal life of the character.
6.  **Physical Delivery**: Employing character-driven gestures and purposeful movement to create an empathetic connection with the audience.
7.  **Energy**: Consistent commitment, physicality, and intention throughout the performance.

## 4. Professional Discipline: The Ego and The Work

A core philosophical pillar of the training program is the strict boundary between personal ego and dramatic collaboration:
- **Attendance & Preparation**: Professionalism requires absolute preparation (lines memorized, scenes developed with strong choices) and punctual attendance.
- **Grace & Generosity**: The classroom operates as a safe space for artistic failure. Actors are expected to actively watch, support, and learn from their peers' breakthroughs.
- **Ego vs. The Work**: Professionalism is defined as the battle between the Ego (desiring personal attention, seeking validation, or refusing notes) and the Work (serving the text and the scene). Actors must check their egos at the door to serve the needs of the play.
